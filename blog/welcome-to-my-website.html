Stop writing “Welcome to my website.” They know.

Your first line is the most valuable thing on your site. Stop wasting it on a doormat.

By Sarah J Hill5 November 20265 min read

“Welcome to my website” is a waste of your best words. People decide in seconds whether to stay, and they read the first two words of a line before deciding whether to read the rest. Spend those words saying what you do, who for, and why you. Not hello.

Why is “Welcome to my website” a waste of time?

Because they know. They clicked the link. They’re here. Telling them where they are is like a shop owner greeting you with “This is a shop.”

Nobody lands on your homepage wanting a welcome. They want one answer: can you sort my problem? Everything that doesn’t answer that is in the way.

How long do I actually get?

About ten seconds. The Nielsen Norman Group, the people who’ve studied web behaviour longer than anyone, found visitors often leave within 10 to 20 seconds. The pages that keep them are the ones that prove their value straight away.

Ten seconds. And you’re spending the first one on “Welcome”.

Does anyone even read my first line?

The first two words of it. People don’t read websites, they scan them, and Nielsen Norman Group found readers often take in just the first two words of a heading before moving on. “Welcome to” burns both.

“Plumber, Cardiff.” “Free counselling.” “Vegan cakes.” Two words. Job done.

Is it hurting me on Google?

Yes. Google builds the title it shows in search results from your page title and your main headings. Its own guidance asks for titles that are descriptive and specific. “Welcome” is neither. Nobody has ever typed “welcome” into Google looking for an electrician.

AI tools are the same. When someone asks “who’s a good web designer in Cardiff?”, they recommend businesses that clearly say what they do and where. They can’t recommend words you never wrote.

What does it say about my business?

That nobody tried. It’s the default line on every cheap template, so it reads as a cheap template.

And if you’re a woman in business, a charity or a community group, you already get sized up before you’ve opened your mouth. A limp first line doesn’t fight that. It agrees with it.

So what do I write instead?

Use this formula. I use it on every site I build:

What you do + who for + where + why you. One line. Then one button.

  • Not “Welcome to Sam’s Electrical.”
    Instead: “Qualified electrician in Cardiff. Rewires, fuse boards and EV chargers, done right first time.”
  • Not “Welcome to our charity.”
    Instead: “Free, confidential mental health support in Cardiff. Turn up. Talk. Be heard.”
  • Not “Welcome to my bakery.”
    Instead: “Vegan celebration cakes in Wisbech, made to order and delivered on the day.”
  • Not “Welcome! We’re passionate about what we do.”
    Instead: “Bookkeeping for sole traders who’d rather be working than doing receipts.”

None of those say welcome. Every one of them is more welcoming, because the visitor knows in two seconds they’re in the right place.

Who gets it right in real life?

Plenty of brands you know. Not one of them opens with “Welcome”.

  • Monzo. Its sign-up page leads with “Apply for an award-winning Monzo account in minutes.” A verb first, so you know what to do. Proof (“award-winning”). A promise (“in minutes”). Ten words, zero waffle.
  • Who Gives A Crap. The toilet paper company that funds sanitation projects. Its own line about itself is “Funny name. Serious business.” Four words that make you smile and make you trust them. That’s ballsy, and it sells loo roll.
  • The ChatShack, Cardiff. A peer-led mental health community I built a site for. The Donate page opens with “Every pound helps someone talk sooner.” It says what your money does before you’ve even looked for the button.
  • This site. “Websites that breathe fire. Not another cookie-cutter site knocked up in a day.” What I do, and what I refuse to do, before you’ve scrolled. I practise what I preach.

Notice the pattern? Specific. Short. Confident. They tell you what you get, not where you are.

What else should be above the fold?

“Above the fold” means what shows on a phone before anyone scrolls. Get these five in and you’re ahead of most sites in your town:

  1. The line. What you do, who for, where. See the formula above.
  2. One button. Call, book or enquire. Pick one. Three buttons means nobody presses any.
  3. One piece of proof. A star rating, “fully insured”, a qualification, or a short quote from a real customer with their first name.
  4. A real photo. You, your team, your actual work. Not a stock model pretending to be you.
  5. Your area. “Cardiff and the Vale”, “Across Fenland”. Local people want local help, and so does Google.

How do I check my own homepage in five minutes?

  1. Open your site on your phone. Look only at what shows before you scroll.
  2. Cover your logo with your thumb. Can a stranger still tell what you do and where?
  3. Read the first two words of your main heading. Do they mean anything on their own?
  4. Find the one obvious next step. If you have to hunt, so do your customers.
  5. Google your business name. Does the title in the results say what you do?

Any “no” is your first fix. It costs nothing but a rewrite.

Can I still be friendly?

Be friendly in how you say it, not in a line that says nothing. “Come as you are” is warmer than any “Welcome” because it shows you get why they came. Want to say hello properly? Do it in your About section, next to a real photo of you, where it actually means something.
