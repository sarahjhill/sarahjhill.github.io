Your website isn’t the problem. Being doubted is.

Nobody tells you they doubted you. They just don’t ring.

By Sarah J Hill12 October 20262 min read

Most small business websites don’t fail because they’re ugly. They fail because a stranger lands on them already half-doubting, and the site gives them nothing to change their mind. Your job is to answer the doubt before it’s spoken: who you are, what you do, proof you’re good at it, and how to get hold of you.

Why are women in business doubted more?

You know the moments. The customer who asks when your husband will be back. The supplier who explains your own trade to you. The networking do where someone assumes you’re the assistant. That doubt doesn’t switch off when people go online. It just goes quiet: a tab closed, a call never made.

You can’t control what people assume. You can control what they find.

What does a doubtful visitor look for first?

Four things, fast, usually on a phone:

  1. Who is this, and is it a real person?
  2. Do they do the thing I need, where I need it?
  3. Are they any good? Who says so?
  4. How do I get hold of them, right now?

If any of those takes more than a scroll to find, you’re relying on their patience. Doubtful people don’t have much.

What actually works?

  • Your face. A real photo of you, not a stock smile. It’s the fastest trust signal there is.
  • Specifics, not adjectives. Your actual qualifications, the number of reviews you have, the towns you cover. “Professional, friendly, reliable” is what everyone types. Facts are what people believe.
  • Prices, or at least “from”. Hide your prices and people assume the worst, then leave without asking.
  • Reviews with names, near the top. Not on a separate page nobody clicks.
  • A way to contact you on every screen. Tap to call, a short form, or both.

What should I cut?

  • “Welcome to my website.” They know. They’re on it.
  • Apology words: just, only, small, try. You wouldn’t say them to a customer’s face. Don’t write them on your homepage.
  • Mission statements nobody asked for.
  • Anything you’d be embarrassed to read out loud across a counter.
“If you wouldn’t say it to a customer’s face, don’t put it on your homepage.” My rule for every site I build

How do I test my own website?

The stranger test. Hand your phone to someone who’s never heard of you. Give them five seconds on your homepage, take it back, and ask: What do I do? Who is it for? What would you do next? If they can’t answer, the site isn’t doing its job. No offence to the site.

Isn’t this just about looking professional?

No. Professional is the minimum. This is about your worth showing up before you do. You’ve done the training, the hours and the hard graft. Your website should make that obvious to a stranger in a hurry, so you never have to prove it from scratch on the phone again.
