Charities: your donate button is in the wrong place.

And it’s probably not the only thing quietly costing you support.

By Sarah J Hill12 October 20262 min read

If someone has to hunt for how to give, they won’t. On most small charity websites the donate button is tucked in a menu, missing on phones, or fighting five other buttons. Put one clear “Donate” in the top bar of every page, visible on a phone without opening a menu, and tell people exactly what their money does.

Where should the donate button go?

  • In the header, on every page, and still visible on a phone. Not hidden inside the burger menu.
  • At the end of every story or update, when people are most moved to help.
  • Straight to a short, simple form. Every extra page between “I want to give” and “done” loses people.

How do I show what a donation actually does?

Turn money into something people can picture. “£5 pays for a hot meal” (use your real numbers) works far harder than “support our work”. Show the people you help, with their consent. Tell one real story at a time, and keep it current. A news page last touched three years ago tells donors nobody’s home.

If you’re a registered charity, remind UK taxpayers about Gift Aid: it adds 25p to every £1 they give, at no extra cost to them. Put the tick box where they can’t miss it.

What do volunteers need from your website?

  • What the roles actually are, how much time they take, and where.
  • A short sign-up form: name, how to reach them, what interests them. Ask the rest later.
  • A named person who’ll reply, and roughly when.

What are the most common mistakes?

  • The annual report as a PDF is the main content. Nobody reads PDFs on a phone.
  • Three different donation platforms linked from different pages. Pick one.
  • Sliding carousels. Nobody waits for slide four.
  • Pale grey text on white. If people can’t read it, they can’t support it. The accessibility standard asks for a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 for normal text.
  • English only, in a Welsh-speaking community. In Wales, offering Cymraeg says you belong.
  • A free template that looks exactly like every other cause asking for the same money.

What does a generic charity template get wrong?

It treats your cause like a product category. The same photo of hands, the same “Make a difference today”, the same three icons. Donors scroll past dozens of those a week. What they don’t see enough of is you: the specific people, the specific place, the specific difference. That’s your edge, and generic design sands it right off.

Where do I start if money is tight?

  1. Move donate into the header and test it on your own phone.
  2. Write one sentence on what £10 does, with your real numbers.
  3. Swap one stock photo for a real one, with permission.
  4. Delete anything out of date.
  5. Ask a stranger to find how to volunteer. Time them.
